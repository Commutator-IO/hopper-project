                  A Machine Reads the Hopper Ledgers:
    Constrained Transcription, Mechanical TEI Export, and Checkability
                        as the Editorial Condition

                                             Michel Hua
                                           Commutator, Paris
                                        michel@commutator.io

         Draft of 13 September 2026, prepared for the Journal of the Text Encoding Initiative



                                                Abstract
      This article reports an experiment in the transcription and encoding of an artist’s record
      books by a large language model under editorial constraints, using the six ledgers of Ed-
      ward and Josephine Hopper at the Whitney Museum of American Art (504 digitised sheets,
      1913–1967) and four of Josephine Hopper’s notebooks from the Sanborn Hopper Archive
      as its corpus. The model (Claude Opus 5 and Claude Fable 5.1) transcribed the sheets from
      the museum’s published photographs into a deliberately restricted LaTeX whose macros
      correspond one to one with elements of a TEI P5 customisation; a mechanical conversion
      produces TEI files that are validated against the customisation in continuous integration;
      scripts derive from the transcriptions, under published rules, an account of prices, mate-
      rials, formats and dated days; and an interface built for the purpose places the reading
      beside the photograph so that any sentence can be checked against the hand it was read
      from. The article describes the corpus, the transcription rules, the encoding and its schema,
      the derived data and the interface, and then discusses the limits of the method: the read-
      ing remains a first pass unchecked by a person, an image-alignment pass on one volume
      reports seventy-four leaves with at least one line the transcription lacks, and the model can-
      not see the colour of ink that carries the cash book’s meaning. The contribution claimed is
      not a finding about Hopper but an editorial condition: a machine reading that is fully cited,
      fully derived and fully exposed to correction. The use of generative AI in the transcription
      and in the drafting of this article is declared.

Keywords. TEI; artist’s ledgers; large language models; transcription; critical apparatus; Ed-
ward Hopper; reproducibility.


1     Introduction
Edward Hopper (1882–1967) recorded his finished work in a set of ledgers that his wife, the
painter Josephine Nivison Hopper (1883–1968), kept from their marriage in 1924 until his
death. Six volumes survive at the Whitney Museum of American Art, accessioned as 96.208
to 96.213, and the museum has digitised and published every sheet with a catalogue descrip-
tor. The volumes are heterogeneous. Three are work books in which Edward Hopper drew a
small ink record of each finished picture with its title and size, and Josephine Hopper wrote
everything else: the paint used, a description, the buyer, the price, the dealer’s commission,
the exhibitions and their verdicts, and the date the cheque cleared. One is a pocket cash book
kept without a gap from 15 November 1913 to 23 March 1967, in a hand that predates the

                                                    1
marriage by eleven years and that the present edition does not attribute. One is a book of
drawings and etching stock-takes, and one is a cash book indexed by dealer. Beside them,
the Whitney’s Sanborn Hopper Archive holds some ninety notebooks of Josephine Hopper’s,
four of which have been digitised: a diary of the marriage as it bore on her own painting, two
working files from the 1947 fight against New York University’s attempt to evict the tenants of
3 Washington Square North, and a two-ring memorandum book of lists.
    The ledgers have been used before. Gail Levin’s catalogue raisonné was written from the
record books and her biography from the diaries (Levin 1995a, 1995b); Deborah Lyons pub-
lished the record books in 1997 (Lyons 1997); Madeleine Larson’s typescript of the Province-
town diaries exists in five parts. What has not existed is a transcription of the digitised sheets
that can be placed beside the photograph of each sheet and checked against it, with an appa-
ratus that states what was read, what was doubted, what could not be read and whose hand
wrote it. This article reports an experiment in producing such a transcription with a large lan-
guage model as the first transcriber, under constraints designed to make its output checkable
rather than trustworthy.
    The objective is methodological. The Hopper corpus was chosen because it is the right
size for a test, because the museum serves its photographs openly at a resolution at which
Josephine Hopper’s figures are legible, and because a ledger is a document in which errors are
measurable: a price either adds up or it does not. The article therefore presents the corpus
(Section 3), the transcription method and its rules (Section 4), the TEI encoding and the
customisation that validates it (Section 5), the interface and the derived data that make the
reading reproducible (Section 6), a discussion of what the method permits and what it does not
(Section 7), and the same method run against three sibling corpora — two of mathematics, one
a filmed lecture — which is where the editorial condition either generalises or fails (Section
8). A longer critical note on the content of the ledgers, drafted by the same model from the
same transcriptions, accompanies the edition and is cited here where a finding illustrates a
point of method (Hua 2026).


2    Related work
Three bodies of work bear on this experiment: the encoding of financial and account records
in TEI, the transcription of handwritten historical documents by large language models, and
the evaluation of such models as producers of TEI markup.
     The TEI encoding of account books has a small but explicit literature. Tomasek and Bau-
man (2013), writing in this journal, proposed a stand-off model of financial records, the
« transactionography », in which transfers between parties are represented as data linked by
pointers to the transcribed document rather than embedded in its markup, so that a transaction
can be encoded once even where the document records it in several places. That distinction
between the document and the transaction is the one the present edition adopts, with a differ-
ent division of labour: the transcription encodes the document and nothing but the document,
and every transaction is recovered from it by a script whose rules are published and whose
output is regenerated on each build (Section 6). The present corpus is also unlike a double-
entry book in that its writer kept no consistent form; the reader that recovers transactions
from Book IV has to change its rule four times across the volume’s fifty-four years, and Section
4 describes why.
     The transcription of handwriting by large language models has moved quickly. Humphries


                                                2
and colleagues (2025) report that, on a corpus of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century English
manuscripts, general-purpose language models achieved character error rates of about six to
seven per cent, an improvement on specialised handwritten text recognition software; Crosilla,
Klic and Colavizza (2025) benchmark several models on the same task and discuss the con-
ditions under which the models’ linguistic priors help and hinder. The present experiment
differs from that work in its object and in its measure. Its object is not running prose but
ruled tables of figures, names and abbreviations, where a language model’s prior is of little
use and may be a liability, since a plausible name is not a correct one and a rounded figure
is a false transaction; and its measure is not an error rate against a ground truth, which does
not exist for these sheets, but the reconciliation of the transcribed figures with the sums the
writer herself pencilled at the foot of the leaves.
    The evaluation of language models as producers of TEI is more recent. Strutz (2026)
proposes a multi-dimensional framework for assessing model-generated TEI, from syntactic
validity to semantic accuracy, on historical correspondence. The present edition takes a differ-
ent position on the question that framework addresses: it does not ask the model to produce
TEI at all. The model writes a restricted transcription language of fourteen macros, and the
TEI is produced from it mechanically, so that the validity of the encoding is a property of the
converter and not of the model, and a difference between the two files is a bug rather than a
judgement. Section 5 sets out the reasoning and Section 7 its cost.

    First, three numbering systems overlap on every sheet and only one of them is an address.
The Hoppers wrote leaf numbers on the paper; the museum’s digitisation counts photographs,
covers and versos included, so that it agrees with the leaf numbers nowhere; and the museum’s
ResourceSpace holds a resource reference that is in no order but that names exactly one image.
« Leaf 58 » names six different things across the six volumes and three photographs within Book
I alone. A citation from the edition therefore prints both the leaf, which is what a reader of
the photograph can see, and the reference, which is the only thing the museum can be asked
about.
    Second, the volumes were written by two people and annotated by more. The inside
front cover of Book I states the division: « Recorded by Jo N Hopper (Mrs. Edward Hopper)
at time each work is finished, or before it leaves studio. Drawings in the 3 books done by

Edward Hopper. » The size in the title line of each work is the one figure Edward Hopper
wrote; everything else in the work books is hers. Book IV is in a hand the edition cannot
place. A transcription that flattened the hands would destroy the archive’s chief documentary
value, and the encoding of hands is accordingly the one element of the apparatus that has no
counterpart in ordinary manuscript transcription and that the method treats as mandatory.
    Third, the colour of ink carries information. In Book IV, from leaf 7 onward, charges are
written in black, receipts in red, and the sums the writer made herself in pencil; on the etching
leaves of Book I, the writer states on the index leaf that ink marks the date a cheque was
received and pencil the date a picture was sold. A transcription in one typeface loses this
unless it records the colour, and a language model reading a photograph does not reliably
distinguish it (Section 7).

3.2   The four notebooks
The four notebooks digitised by the Whitney are listed in Table 2. They are not of one kind.
Garrulities + Grouch is a diary of twenty-two dated entries between 1927 and 1959, with the
dates in the margin and the entries not in chronological order. Re: 3 Wash Sq is a working file
of drafts and a chronicle, in which the drafts were written from the front of the pad and the
chronicle from the back, so that the two meet in the middle and the chronicle runs backwards
through the photographs. Battle of Wash Sq. is a memorandum book of eight sheets. The
black two-ring notebook is a book of lists, whose dated content runs from 1937 to 1964 and
whose subject changes every page or two. Their encoding required one element the ledgers
do not: a dated entry, with the writer’s date and the transcriber’s assigned date kept apart.

3.3   Provenance and access
The three work books were the gift of Lloyd Goodrich, who organised the Whitney’s Hopper
retrospectives of 1950 and 1964; Book IV and the dealers’ book were purchases. The notebooks
came with the Sanborn Hopper Archive in 2017. Twenty-two further diaries of 1933 to 1956
are at the Provincetown Art Association and Museum, which publishes Madeleine Larson’s
typescript of them; those are not transcribed here and are used only through the typescript,
cited by part and page.
    The edition stores no photograph. The Whitney’s download endpoint answers cross-origin
requests and honours byte ranges; the interface points an image element at the museum’s file,
fetched as it is looked at, and the repository refuses image files. The inventory of sheets was
harvested by a person in a browser, because the museum’s listing pages sit behind a bot-check
that the edition does not circumvent, and the harvest is committed as text so that it can be
reviewed in a diff.

4     The transcription method
4.1   The unit of work and the model
A batch is twelve sheets, read in one fresh model context, and written as a single LaTeX file
whose header records the model, the date, and what the batch found difficult. The skill that
performs the reading accepts three models, Claude Fable 5.1, Claude Fable 5 and Claude Opus
5, and refuses any other, on the ground that a pass made on a model nobody chose produces a
file whose provenance is an accident. Every file records the model that read its sheets, so that
provenance is a fact about the file and not about the session. Between 5 and 13 September
2026, Opus 5 read most of the ledgers and the first sittings of the notebooks; Fable 5.1 read
the last sittings of the black notebook and the Battle of Wash Sq. Where the rule of one context
per batch was not kept, the header says so: batches 3 and 4 of Book I shared a context.

4.2   The rules
The governing rule is stated in the edition’s method and is specific to ledgers: a guessed figure
is a sale that did not happen. An invented word in a letter is a bad reading; an invented price
in a ledger is a transaction at a price nobody paid, to a buyer who never bought, and it will be
cited, because a table looks like data in a way that prose does not. From that rule follow the
others.
     Nothing illegible is guessed. A passage that cannot be read is marked illegible, and the mark
is never replaced by a plausible reading. A reading offered with doubt is marked doubtful.
Nothing is expanded or normalised: « Inv. » stays « Inv. », « 30 - 1/3 » stays as written, and
a name spelt four ways in the archive stays spelt four ways, because a corrected name is a
claim about who bought a picture that the leaf did not make. A key to the abbreviations
and notations is published separately, and each of its entries must match a transcribed leaf or
the build fails. What the writer struck out is kept and marked struck, since a crossing-out is
often the more informative half of an entry. Marginal additions are transcribed as marginal,
with their position. The transcriber’s own observations are confined to a note element that is
typographically and structurally distinct from the writer’s text, so that a count over the corpus
can exclude it; the materials census, for example, reads only the writer’s words, because the
transcriber’s notes discuss materials constantly and a count that read them would report a rare
colourman as common because somebody remarked on its rarity.
     Hands are attributed to Josephine Hopper, to Edward Hopper, to a later writer, or to nobody.
Unidentified is a permitted answer and is, the method notes, the right one far more often than
confidence suggests. The drawn lettering of the covers is unattributed; the whole of Book IV is
unattributed; a pencil annotation on Book II leaf 46 in a heavier hand is attributed to nobody.
Where a model attributes a hand, it does so on the letterforms and says so.
     There is one edition and no second register. The project this method derives from carries a
modernised text beside the diplomatic one; a ledger needs none, since its English is plain and
its columns are already a table, and a second text would be a second artefact to keep in step.

4.3   What the model wrote
The model writes a restricted LaTeX defined in a preamble of fourteen macros (Table 3). The
restriction is deliberate. A converter that accepted arbitrary LaTeX would silently mangle what
it did not understand, and a silently mangled ledger is a table of numbers that look right; the

renderer accepts only the subset and fails loudly outside it, naming the file, the line and the
construct. It also refuses a resource reference that is not in the harvested inventory and a file
that omits the rights notice.

5     The TEI encoding
5.1   Why the TEI is derived and not written
The TEI files are produced by a script from the transcriptions alone. The script re-reads no
sheet, and the method requires that it must not: the macros map one to one onto TEI ele-
ments, the conversion is mechanical, and a difference between a transcription and its TEI is by
construction a defect in the converter and not an editorial decision. This is the reverse of the
usual division, in which the TEI is the source of record and a display is derived from it, and it
was chosen for three reasons.
    The first is the model. A language model can be asked to write TEI and will do so fluently,
and its fluency is the problem: it will produce well-formed markup whose structure is plau-
sible and whose attribute values are invented, and the failure has no structural signature. A
fourteen-macro language has a small surface, each macro answers one editorial question, and
a macro used wrongly is visible as a wrong answer to that question rather than as a subtly
wrong element.
    The second is the checker. The transcription is read by people beside the photograph, in a
rendering the converter also produces from the same file, and the rendering has to be legible
to a reader who knows nothing of XML. A LaTeX source with fourteen macros is also readable
in itself, in the way a diplomatic transcription in a printed edition is, and can be corrected by
a person with a text editor.
    The third is validation. Because the TEI is derived, it can be validated on every build against
a customisation that declares exactly the elements the converter emits, and a validation failure
identifies a converter bug or a macro misuse without any ambiguity about whose judgement
is at fault.

5.2   The customisation
The customisation is an ODD, hopper.odd, served at the URL every exported file names, from
which both the schema and its documentation derive. It declares fifty-five elements, and the
fifty-five are not a sample of TEI’s six hundred but the exact reach of the conversion: every
element exists because a macro maps onto it. The modules and elements included are shown
in Table 4. Five attribute value lists are declared closed, so that a @type on a division, a
figure, a note, a term or a rendition value on a highlighted cell is drawn from a list and
not invented per file, and the validator refuses any other value. The value lists are: division
types (leaf, work, entry, part, editorial); figure types (record-sketch, clipping); note types
(editorial); the keyword facets (person, place, collection, dealer, society, publication,
prize, medium, client, subject, feature, hopper); and renditions (bold, italic, mono, ink-red,
ink-pencil, ink-blue, rule-above).
     The header of each file carries the provenance that the method requires. The two au-
thors are named in the order the inside cover gives them; the responsibility statement names
the model that read the sheets and the date, and states that the pass is a first machine pass

5.4   The notebooks
A diary entry is a division of type entry whose head carries the writer’s date as written and
the assigned date in the @when attribute. The two are kept apart because the assignment is
the one editorial claim an entry carries: Garrulities has entries whose day is a blot and whose
year is inferred from the run of dates around them, and the encoding must not present an
inference as a reading. The following is the black notebook’s page 64, the one sentence of
Edward Hopper’s stated purpose that the ten books preserve, with the writer’s own recasting

5.5   Validation
The converter’s output is validated in continuous integration against the schema derived from
the ODD, and the workflow fails on any invalid file. The validator exists because of a defect
it found: for as long as the export was checked only for well-formedness, every file in the
corpus was well-formed XML and invalid TEI, since the converter had placed a head at each
leaf inside a single division per batch, and TEI places a head only at the start of the division it
heads. Nothing had said so because nothing had asked. The lesson is general and is recorded
in the validator’s own header: a deposit of unvalidated TEI is a deposit of XML.

6     Interface and reproducibility
6.1    The reading beside the photograph
The interface at https://hopper.commutator.io was built for this project and does one thing
that no general tool did: it renders the transcription in a left pane and the Whitney’s photo-
graph of the sheet in a right pane, and scrolling the transcription past a page-break element
turns the photograph to that sheet. Every doubtful figure can be checked against the hand it
was read from without a second window. A Cite control on each sheet builds three citations
from what the page already knows, a sheet, a leaf and a batch, each naming the museum’s
object clause and the edition’s reading clause separately, and offers only those that exist: a
sheet the Hoppers did not number gets no leaf citation, because inventing a number for the
sake of a tidy citation would invent a fact about the object. The citation names the model and
the date of the reading, because a citation of this edition cites a reading and not a fact, and a
reading that could not be dated could not be superseded.
    The reading views for the notebooks work the same way and are addressed by the mu-
seum’s reference rather than by a page number, since an opening photographed whole carries
two page numbers and a cover none.

6.2    Derived data, and the rule that a build must fail
Everything the interface shows beyond the transcription is derived from the transcription by
a script whose header states what it counts and what it therefore is not, and the continuous
integration workflow regenerates each derived file and fails if the committed copy differs. The
manifest of what is transcribed is observed from the files, never declared, so that a progress
figure cannot claim work that was not done; the only declared states, checked and skipped, are
held in a separate file that is empty. The citation metadata is generated from a census of the
files, on the ground that a hand-written sheet count is wrong from the next batch onward. The
glossary of abbreviations and notations is hand-declared, but each entry carries a pattern that
must match a transcribed leaf, and the search runs over the writer’s words and never over a
transcriber’s note, so that an entry cannot cite as evidence the sentence written because of it.
    The accounts reader recovers transactions from the ruled rows. It counts a row only where
the leaf puts a price in one cell and a date in another, because the column a date stands in
is what says whether it dates the sale or the cheque; on the etching leaves of Book I the first
column dates the exhibition, so those rows are set aside rather than dated wrongly. Where
a row states a price, a rate and a receipt, the receipt is checked against the price less the
commission; of ninety-five such rows, ninety agree to the cent, and the five that do not are
reported and not corrected. For Book IV the reader has a second set of rules, set out period
by period in a method note, because the writer’s form changes four times across the volume:
items are the charge; a bill counts only where nothing was itemised since the last settlement; a
bare figure is a subtotal where the arithmetic of the lines above it says so, since the leaf rules a
line above a subtotal and the transcription does not record the rule; a « less » line is a deduction
whichever thing it names; a title charged more than once with a payment on account is one
picture at the largest figure. The check on the reader is the book’s own pencil totals, and the
reader’s receipts reconcile to them to the cent wherever the leaf’s arithmetic closes.
    Two of the reader’s rules were found by bugs, and the method note records them: an
unanchored match on the word bill took a story title, « Wild Bill in Deadwood Gulch », for an
invoice and lost a drawing of 1915; a header rule read « May 25th Initial Letters » as a new


                                                10
client and counted a bill of 225 twice. Both were caught by comparing per-year totals with
leaves read by hand. A faithful transcription can surface a reader bug without containing one.

6.3   The rules, stated as proofs
A reader whose rules are written in prose and applied by a script can drift from them unnoticed,
and a total can reconcile by accident. The derived accounts are therefore also restated in the
Lean 4 theorem prover, in a directory of the repository that depends on nothing but Lean,
so that the continuous integration build fails if the script and its stated rules disagree. The
formalisation was written by Claude Opus 5 under the author’s direction, on 14 September
2026. Every figure in the ledgers is dollars and cents and every commission a third, a quarter,
a tenth or fifteen per cent, so money is represented as integers in sixtieths of a cent, where
all of them are exact. General lemmas are proved for every input by decision procedures for
linear arithmetic; the data is checked by evaluation in the kernel (decide +kernel) and never
by compiled code; and warnings are errors, so an unfinished proof fails the build.
    Two certificates are proved. The first concerns the accounts as published: each activity’s
years are recomputed from its rows, and the identity that what accrued equals what was
collected plus what is outstanding plus what was not recorded is derived from the rows rather
than read off the totals; the reader’s two-cent tolerance is proved to accept every figure written
to the cent after a truncated third and to reject a slip of a third of a dollar; the ninety-five
checkable rows are proved to yield ninety agreements and exactly the five disagreements the
reader reports; and each hand-declared pencil total is proved equal to the reader’s receipts
for that year plus the declared omissions. The second concerns the Book IV reader. Its four
passes are stated as Lean functions, and the properties the method note asserts in prose are
proved of them for every input: a bill is charged only where nothing was itemised since the
last settlement, so no commission is counted twice; a « less » line is never a charge; a bare
figure is a subtotal exactly when the answering line or the arithmetic says so; a picture paid
in instalments is charged once; the year does not carry across a gap between batches. The
kernel then evaluates the functions over all 3,281 transcribed rows of the volume and proves
that each row is decided exactly once, that the Lean reader and the JavaScript reader agree
on every row’s kind, year and disposition, and that the published counters are counts of those
decisions. The JavaScript exports, for each row, the lexical features it recognised and the
decisions it took; Lean recomputes the decisions from the features and proves equality row by
row. Altering a single disposition in the exported data causes the build to fail.
    The exercise refined one rule, since the proof required the exception that a « less » line
which is also a ditto immediately under a receipt reads as that receipt, and it turned a known
gap into a theorem: the absence of 1921 from the cash book follows from a year line on leaf
53 that reads 1920 over entries of 1921, and the corresponding theorem is written to fail
once that line is corrected at source. The limits are those of the method as a whole. The
certificates say nothing about a reading, and a figure misread consistently in the rows and
in the sums would pass; the regular expressions that recognise a row’s words are inputs, not
proved; and what is proved is that the script agrees with its stated rules, not that the rules are
the right interpretation of the writer’s practice. The certificates are checked on every push by
the workflow that builds the site, and an equivalent GitLab CI configuration is provided.

7.2   Limits of the model as a transcriber and as an encoder
The experiment identified five limits, none of them a matter of error rate.
    The model does not see colour reliably. Book IV’s meaning is in its ink, and fourteen
batches were transcribed without recording it; the colour was recovered afterwards by an
image-processing script that classifies each money cell and writes the result into the transcrip-
tion, on the ground that colour is the one thing on the leaf a machine can see better than the
hand it is written in. A method that depended on the language model for the ink would have
produced a cash book without a status field.
    The model carries context forward as a rule, and rules fail at boundaries. The year of a
Book IV entry is stated once, on a line of its own, and carried down the leaf; where leaf 53’s
year line reads « 1920 » above entries of 1921, the transcription keeps the line as written and
the reader carries the year, so that 1921’s income sits under 1920 in every table. This is the
intended behaviour of both, and it is wrong, and it is listed for correction at source rather than
repaired in the derived data.
    The model reads names as language. The archive spells Crowninshield three ways, Saltillo
four and Keppel two, and the model, left to itself, would settle them. The rule that nothing
is normalised holds it to the leaf, and the glossary records the variants; but the discipline is
the rule’s and not the model’s, and a reader who relaxed it would get a tidier and less truthful
archive.
    The model is fluent in TEI, and fluency is the hazard. Asked to produce markup it produces
plausible markup, and a plausible attribute value is not a schema-valid one. The decision not
to let the model write TEI at all, and to derive the TEI mechanically from a fourteen-macro
language it can hardly misuse, is the central encoding decision of the edition. Its cost is that
the encoding is only as expressive as the macro set, and the macro set was designed for what
these ten books contain; the transactionography of Tomasek and Bauman (2013), for instance,
has no place in the transcription and lives entirely in the reader.
    The model is confident about hands. The attribution of a hand on the letterforms is the
one judgement in the apparatus that no arithmetic can check, and the model attributes readily.
The rule that unidentified is a permitted answer, and the decision to attribute the whole of Book
IV to nobody, are the method’s response; a human palaeographer with the physical volumes
would do better, and the edition says so on the leaf.

7.3   Human validation
The edition distinguishes three states of a batch, and only one of them is observed from the
files. Drafted is what a file’s existence proves. Skipped, for a batch judged to hold nothing to
transcribe, and checked, for a batch that a person has compared with the photographs sheet by
sheet, are declared in a status file, and that file declares nothing for any batch. The reason is
stated in its comment: a hand-kept status table drifts optimistic and nobody notices, so the two
states no file can prove are the only two a person may write, and the site shows a transcribed
batch as drafted because that is observed rather than declared.
     What a human check consists of has been defined as an open question of the edition rather
than assumed. The protocol under discussion is the one this article’s comparison table per-
forms in miniature: a person opens the reading beside the photograph, reads each transcribed
line against the sheet, corrects the transcription at source with a note that names who checked
it, and only then may the batch be declared checked; the citation of every sheet in that batch
then changes to say that a person has gone through it, and only a person may declare that.

                                               16
Until then, every citation of the edition names a machine pass and a date, and the address of
the sheet does not move when the reading does.

7.4    What the method permits, and what it does not
The method permits a reading of a five-hundred-sheet archive to be produced in days, cited
by leaf and reference, exported as valid TEI, and exposed in full to correction beside its source.
It permits derived data to be regenerated from the reading under published rules and to be
checked, where the writer left sums, against the writer’s own arithmetic. It permits the rules
by which that data is derived to be stated as functions in a theorem prover, their properties
proved for every input, and the script that applies them proved to agree with them on every
row. It permits the whole to be held in one repository whose every change is a dated commit,
so that a citation names a version and a later reader can see what was read on the day it was
cited.
    It does not produce a checked transcription, and it does not claim to. It does not see
ink, and it does not attribute hands with authority. It does not, in its present form, encode
transactions, and it cannot produce a text more expressive than its macro language. And it
does not remove the need for the person the method was designed around: the reader beside
the photograph, whose correction is the only event that changes a batch’s state.


8     The method against four corpora
The apparatus described here was not built for this corpus alone. Three sibling editions run on
the same tooling — the same restricted LaTeX, the same rule that an unrecognised construct
fails the build, the same reading placed beside its witness — and they were chosen to differ
in the two things that matter: what the document is, and what kind of witness stands behind
it. Two are archives of art, two of mathematics; three are written and one is spoken. Table 6
gives their state, read from the four repositories on 19 September 2026.

decision against a second edition here (Section 4.2) is a decision this project had to take twice,
in opposite directions. The Gallica corpus made the address itself the unit: a volume enters
only where the library serves the whole of it, and a manuscript that Gallica merely references
on a partner’s site is in no table and no thumbnail, which is the same rule this edition states
about documents with no public digitisation. And the morphology course made the witness
something other than an image.
    That last case is the one that generalises the claim. Debord’s course is seventy-seven hours
of speech; the first transcription is not the model’s but the platform’s automatic captions, and
the model’s work is an index — 95 terms, 73 references and 139 digressions, each carrying
the second at which the thing is said. The repository publishes no caption and no video: only
video identifiers, term identifiers and offsets in seconds, the captions themselves staying out of
version control, as the photographs stay out of this one. The editorial condition is unchanged
and its instrument moves: a reader does not compare the reading with a photograph but with
the recording, at the timestamp given, by listening. What every corpus here has in common is
not a document type; it is that no sentence is asserted which a reader cannot address.
    The platform corpus also shows where the condition begins to fail, and it is worth stating
before such corpora multiply. A sheet at the Whitney will answer to the same reference in
twenty years; a video can be withdrawn, re-edited, or have its captions regenerated by a newer
recogniser that yields different words at different seconds. The address survives and what it
serves does not, and nothing in the citation announces the change. Three things follow, none
of them solved here: a citation of such a witness has to carry the date it was consulted and a
fingerprint of what was consulted — a hash of the caption file, a duration, a count of segments
— enough to prove that the witness moved without republishing what may not be republished;
a verification of this kind is revocable, and an interface that keeps a dead timestamp on screen
is asserting something it can no longer support; and the rights run the opposite way from a
heritage institution’s, since a platform forbids the mirror while promising no permanence. An
edition may not copy what nobody undertakes to keep.


9    Conclusion
This article has described an edition in which a language model is the first transcriber and the
editorial apparatus is designed to make its reading checkable rather than trustworthy. The
method rests on four decisions: a restricted transcription language whose macros answer one
editorial question each; a mechanical derivation of TEI from that language, validated on every
build against a customisation that declares exactly what the converter emits; a derivation of all
counts and figures from the transcription under published rules, checked against the writer’s
own sums and, for the accounts, certified in Lean against the rules as stated; and an interface
that places the reading beside the photograph and cites every sheet by the museum’s own
reference and the edition’s own dated pass.
    The corpus, the Hopper ledgers and notebooks, was the occasion for the experiment and
is a good one, because a ledger’s errors are measurable and because the museum serves the
photographs openly. The critical note that accompanies the edition (Hua 2026) reads the
transcription whole and is offered as a demonstration of what the method produces at first
pass, its errors left in view.
    What the experiment opens is a division of labour. The model reads; the scripts derive; the
schema validates; and the person checks, corrects at source, and declares. The first three have


                                               18
been done for this corpus. The fourth has not, and the edition’s own status file, which declares
nothing, is the measure of how far the method has come and where it stops. Two extensions are
planned: the deposit of the corpus with a persistent identifier, and the definition and execution
of the human check that turns a drafted batch into a checked one. Both are tracked as issues
of the repository, and the second is the one on which every claim in this article depends.
