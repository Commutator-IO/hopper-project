\chapter{What the pictures were made of, and what shape}

\section{The formula}

From Book II onward nearly every oil leaf carries, under the title, a short block naming the colourman, the canvas and its priming, the white, the oil and the thinner, in that order and in the same words for thirty years. Of 270 work headings in the transcribed leaves, 77 carry a formula. It is the only surviving statement of what was on the palette on a given day, and it is not a measurement of an object or a report of an event: it is, as the edition's technique page says, \q{her hand recording his practice}.

The materials, counted once per work and with spellings folded (Chremnitz, Cremnitz and Crimnitz are one white; Winsor and Windsor one colourman; turpentine, tarpentine and terpentine one bottle), fall into six groups. The colourmen: Rembrandt on seventeen works, all in Book II and all around 1932; Blockx on eighteen, 1939 to 1949; Winsor \& Newton on fifty-five, 1939 to 1964; Grumbacher once, for a canvas in 1944. The whites: zinc white on nineteen works, 1929--32; lead white on twelve, 1943--57; flake white on thirty, 1944--64; silver white on eight, around 1949, which she once glosses \q{Blockx Silver (lead) White}; Chremnitz white on nine, 1952--56. The oils: poppy on twenty-six works, 1932--44; linseed on forty-five, 1943--64; the leaves never remark on the change. Turpentine on forty. The canvas makers: Winton on eight, Fornet (or Foinet) on two, \q{(National)} on twelve between 1949 and 1955, Herga on ten from 1956 to 1963. The priming: double on nineteen works to 1955, single on thirteen from 1949. Table~\ref{tab:materials} in the appendix gives the counts.

Put together, the formulae make a chronology of practice that the leaves state and nobody has stated for them. In 1932: \q{Rembrandt, zinc white, poppy oil}, fourteen works. In 1937--38: \q{Rembrandt colors, lead white, linseed oil} on Belgian canvas. From 1939: Winsor \& Newton with Blockx, silver or zinc white, poppy oil, on Winton or Belgian double prime. From 1943 to 1948: Winsor \& Newton, flake white, linseed oil, turpentine. From 1949 to 1951: the same on \q{(National)} double prime. From 1952 to 1955: Chremnitz white in place of flake, still on National double prime. From 1956: Herga canvas, single prime, flake white again, and, under the last picture, lead white. The one dated change in the whole sequence is the colourman, and the Provincetown diary supplies the date: on 18 May 1938, \q{E. wants to change over to Windsor Newton oils --- So I'm to inherit gold Rembrandt colors.} The ledgers show the change a leaf later, on New York Movie in January 1939, \q{Winton canvas, Winsor \& Newton colors. --- 1st lead white and linseed oil. 2nd Zinc white} (\lf{Book II}{27}{16621}). The leaf also shows what a formula can carry that a list cannot: two whites on one picture, the second over the first.

Two things should be said against reading too much into this. The formula is present on only seventy-seven works, and a work is dated by it only where she wrote \q{Painted in \ldots} in the same statement or he put the year in the title line, so that the chronology rests on forty-three dated works and not on all of them. And what the leaf says is what she wrote, which is what he told her or what she saw on the table; \q{Colors and canvas unknown, probably zinc white} under November, Washington Square (\lf{Book III}{67}{16582}) is the one admission of not knowing, and it concerns a canvas of 1932 whose sky was repainted in 1959. The formula is a record of a habit, not a laboratory report, and the two agree only as far as the habit was regular. It was very regular.

\section{The size}

The size in the title line is the one figure in the archive Edward Hopper wrote himself, and it is the only quantity that measures an object rather than reporting an event. Read from the headings only, 199 of 270 carry a size, and they make 78 formats: 72 landscape, 6 portrait, none square. Thirty-four formats recur; forty-four occur once. Nothing is rounded, so 23 1/2 by 28 and 23 1/2 by 28 3/4 are two formats because they are two things he wrote.

The recurring formats are the watercolour sheets. Twenty-two works at 20 by 28 and twenty at 14 by 20, the whole and the half of the imperial sheet, are the Cape and Vermont watercolours of Books II and III: Cobb House, Longnook Valley, Mouth of Pamet, White River at Sharon, Oregon Coast, Jo in Wyoming. Nine at 12 by 18 are the Gloucester drawings of Book V. The oils cluster differently. Seven at 24 by 29 are the early canvases of Book I, Corner Saloon, House by a Railroad, Sailing, Moonlight Interior; six at 23 1/2 by 28 3/4 are the Paris oils. Seven at 28 by 40 are the studio pictures of 1949 to 1954, High Noon, Conference at Night, Morning Sun, Portrait of Orleans, Office in a Small City; six at 30 by 40 are 7 A.M., Sea Watchers, South Carolina Morning, Excursion into Philosophy, Sunlight on Brownstones, Two Puritans; four at 29 by 40 are Light House Hill, Rooms by the Sea, Sun in an Empty Room and Ex Lax, a format he used in 1927 and again in 1963; and four at 40 by 60 are the largest pictures of the last decade, People in the Sun, Sunlight in a Cafeteria, A Woman in the Sun, Intermission. Nighthawks is alone at 33 by 60, and Blackwell's Island and Seventh Avenue Shops, which is Early Sunday Morning, share 35 by 60. The six portrait formats are all Mexican watercolours of 1943 and 1946, Saltillo Mansion, Monterrey Cathedral, the Sierra Madre, turned upright for the mountains.

What the sizes show, beyond the obvious, is that the museums' measurements and his agree to within the stretcher. The Metropolitan's Tables for Ladies is 48 1/4 by 60 3/8 against his \q{4 x 5 ft.}; the Art Institute's Nighthawks 33 1/8 by 60 against his 33 by 60; MoMA's House by the Railroad 24 by 29 to the inch. Where they do not agree, as with the Des Moines Automat at 28 1/8 by 35 against both ledgers' 28 by 36, the discrepancy is an inch, and the ledger is the earlier measurement.

\section{The frames, from the other book}

The ledgers stop at the canvas. The black notebook goes on to the frame, and it does so for her pictures and not his, which is itself a fact about the household: the man whose canvases went to Rehn's was framed by the gallery, and the woman whose canvases hung in the rear studio bought her own frames from Burges-Revel of La Porte, Indiana, at \$21 to \$30.75 each, and wrote down which picture went in which. The Rehn frames appear in her lists as objects in the rack, \q{2 gold from Rehn Gal. 28 1/4 x 36 1/2}, \q{Silver frame f/ Canvas from Rehn Gal. / 37 1/2 x 23 1/4 Canvas = 36 x 25}, and in Book IV as deductions on the dealer's statement: \q{Frame for Dauphinee House -48 00}, \q{Frame by Kulicke for “East River” 144 00}. What she thought of frames on his pictures is in Book III: \q{Bad frame; insert same color as window curtain R., takes all force out of highest spot in canvas} under A Woman in the Sun, and, at the very end, \q{the only perfect frame ever worn by a Hopper canvas}, on the same picture, secured by the woman who bought it.
