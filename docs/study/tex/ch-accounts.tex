\chapter{The money, added up}

\section{What can be counted, and what cannot}

The figures in this chapter are read out of the transcribed rows by a script whose rules are published with the edition, and they were generated on 11 September 2026 from 46 batches and 543 sheets. They are floors. Every leaf not yet transcribed contributes nothing, and every figure moves as batches land. Three limits are structural and should be stated before any number is.

First, the ruling. A row is counted only where the leaf puts the price in one cell and the date in another, because the column a date stands in is what says whether it dates the sale or the cheque. The etchings leaves of Book I date the exhibition in their first column and carry an impression's whole history on one line, so their sales have no date but the day the cheque cleared, and 201 rows are reported as unparsed rather than dated wrongly. Book III rules no columns and writes the sale as a sentence, \q{6000 - 1/3 = 4000. Feb. 17}, and is read for that sentence and nothing else. Book V's money is on the sheets and in no total.

Second, the two trades. Books I, II, III and the dealers' book record Edward Hopper selling his own work; Book IV records him being paid for drawings and, from 1920, records the pictures again from the dealer's side. The three are kept apart. Adding the illustration income to the art would change every total without changing its label, and adding Book IV's cash to the work books' accruals would count most pictures twice.

Third, the three buckets. A sale is \emph{collected} where its row states a receipt, \emph{outstanding} where it states a receipt still to come, and \emph{not recorded} where the row says nothing either way. Reading silence as debt would compare a silence with a sum. Book IV names a cheque for nearly every charge; the work books often name none.

\section{The illustrator, 1913--1925}

Table~\ref{tab:ill} gives the illustration decade. Charged, 14,455.87 dollars over the thirteen calendar years from 1913 to 1925; received, 14,654.87; the balance negative by 199, because the book receipts money it never charged, the Shipping Board's three hundred for \q{Smash the Hun} being the largest instance. The best years are 1920, at 2950 charged, and 1917--18, at about 1500 each; the worst is 1915, at 500. There is no row for 1921, and the reason is on leaf 53 of the book: its year line reads \q{1920} above entries that are 1921's, the transcription keeps the line as written, and the reader carries the year from the last one the volume states, so 1921's income sits under 1920 in the table. It is the kind of defect the method is designed to expose rather than repair silently, and it is listed for correction at source. These are not large sums even for the time, and they are the whole of what the paintings books do not mention. By 1925 the picture sales through Rehn, in the same pocket book, had passed them.

\section{The painter, by the work books}

Table~\ref{tab:art} gives the art side as the work books state it, year by year: sales counted, gross, commission, net, and what the rows say was receipted. The rows that can be read this way number 380, and they carry 270,914 dollars of net over forty-four years; 62,777 of it is receipted on the row, none is stated as outstanding, and 208,137 is not recorded, which is to say that Book II and Book III write one date to a sale and do not separate the day the work went out from the day the cheque came. Every art sale whose row states a receipt was eventually paid.

The curve is the career. A few hundred dollars a year to 1925; three to five thousand a year from 1926 to 1929; 12,252 net in 1930, the year of Tables for Ladies to the Metropolitan and Early Sunday Morning to the Whitney; then the Depression, 1765 in 1931, 920 in 1932, 57 in 1933; three or four thousand a year through the late 1930s and 1940s, with the shape depending on whether a museum bought; 9853 in 1954; 14,940 in 1956, 17,647 in 1957; 20,667 in 1962 and 21,000 in each of 1963 and 1964; 16,667 in 1966 for one picture. Ninety of ninety-five checkable rows reconcile receipt to price less commission to the cent; the five that do not are reported and not corrected. One sale, Blackwell's Island, is written into two volumes and counted once.

Table~\ref{tab:works} ranks the sales that name a work. Every one of the top ten is in Book III and dated 1959 or later; the eleventh, Girlie Show, is the Book II canvas of 1941 sold in 1961. The first sale above 3000 dollars is Tables for Ladies in 1930; the next is Cape Cod Afternoon in 1938; nothing else passes that figure until Rooms for Tourists in 1946. Table~\ref{tab:buyers} ranks the buyers the transcription tags as persons or collections, on the rows that name them: Charles Stein, the Huntington Hartford Foundation, Stephen Clark, the Metropolitan, the Whitney, the Carnegie, Mrs. Blanchard of the 1924 watercolours. Table~\ref{tab:dealers} gives the dealers by the rows that name them beside a price: Rehn first, Keppel most often, and Goodspeed's of Boston second by value on the strength of one canvas.

\section{The painter, by the cash book}

Table~\ref{tab:cash} gives Book IV as a cash book for the pictures from 1920 to 1967, the charges the leaves itemise against the cheques they receipt. It is a different number from the work books' and it should be, because it is the dealer's statement and not the studio's record: 544,547 charged against 432,871 received over forty-seven years, the difference mostly the dealer's third and the instalments. Its shape agrees with the work books' where the two overlap and is fuller where they do not. The 1920s peak at 11,444 charged in 1928; the 1930s trough at 77 in 1933 and recover to 10,032 in 1938; the 1940s run between 1590 and 11,383; the 1950s climb from 4790 in 1950 to 39,457 in 1956; the 1960s reach 46,517 in 1962 and 47,982 in 1963, then 32,820, 25,100, 25,000, and 8100 in the year he died. The receipts, which are the figures her pencil sums check, run 40,218 in 1962 and 25,657 in 1963. In no year of his life did Edward Hopper's cheques from his dealer exceed forty-one thousand dollars, and in only nine years did they exceed twenty.

The reader knows what it cannot read. Fifty-eight subtotals, 124 deductions, 81 restated nets, 93 carried sums and six rubbed-out drafts are excluded by rule; five instalment sales are collapsed to one picture each; thirteen rows have no description. Where the year's pencil total closes, the receipts reconcile to the cent. Where the transcription and the leaf disagree, as on leaf 156, where the note reads the fore-edge total as 21,949 and the marginal as 21,944, the leaf needs re-reading and this chapter says so rather than choosing.

\section{The days the books were written on}

One more count, because it is about the record rather than the money. Of the rows in the six ledgers whose date resolves to a calendar day, 2146 fall on 1204 distinct days between 1913 and 1967; Book IV supplies 1287 of them, the dealers' book 478, Book I 358, and Book II, which writes one date to a sale, only two. The busiest day in the archive is 18 October 1929, with twenty-nine rows across Book I and the dealers' book, a Keppel and Kennedy settlement eleven days before the crash; the longest run of consecutive days is seven, 2 to 8 February 1929, the week of the second Rehn show. The rows fall on every day of the week but Sunday, which has 34 against about two hundred for each of the others; and they fall in every month, with a marked hollow in July, August and September, which is the Cape. Table~\ref{tab:streak} gives the years. These are the days the books record a transaction, not the days anybody worked, and Josephine Hopper wrote almost all of them.
