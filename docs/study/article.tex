\begin{abstract}
\noindent This article reports an experiment in the transcription and encoding of an artist's record books by a large language model under editorial constraints, using as its corpus the six ledgers of Edward and Josephine Hopper at the Whitney Museum of American Art (504 digitised sheets, 1907--1967) and four of Josephine Hopper's notebooks. The model (Claude Opus 5 and Claude Fable 5.1) transcribed the sheets from the museum's published photographs into a deliberately restricted LaTeX whose macros correspond one to one with elements of a TEI P5 customisation; a mechanical conversion produces TEI files that are validated against the customisation in continuous integration; scripts derive from the transcriptions, under published rules, an account of prices, materials, formats and dated days; and an interface built for the purpose places the reading beside the photograph so that any sentence can be checked against the hand it was read from. The article describes the corpus, the transcription rules, the encoding and its schema, the derived data and the interface, and then the limits: the reading is a first pass unchecked by a person, an alignment pass on one volume reports seventy-four leaves with at least one line the transcription lacks, and the model cannot see the ink colour that carries the cash book's meaning. The contribution is not a finding about Hopper but an editorial condition: a machine reading fully cited, fully derived and fully exposed to correction. The use of generative AI in the transcription and in the drafting is declared.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords.} artist's ledgers; large language models; transcription; critical apparatus; hand attribution; Edward Hopper; reproducibility.

\section{Introduction}

Edward Hopper (1882--1967) recorded his finished work in a set of ledgers that his wife, the painter Josephine Nivison Hopper (1883--1968), kept from their marriage in 1924 until his death. Six volumes survive at the Whitney Museum of American Art, accessioned as 96.208 to 96.213, and the museum has digitised and published every sheet with a catalogue descriptor (Whitney Museum of American Art, n.d.). The volumes are heterogeneous. Three are work books in which Edward Hopper drew a small ink record of each finished picture with its title and size, and Josephine Hopper wrote everything else: the paint used, a description, the buyer, the price, the dealer's commission, the exhibitions and their verdicts, and the date the cheque cleared. One is a pocket cash book kept without a gap from 15 November 1913 to 23 March 1967, in a hand that predates the marriage by eleven years and that the present edition does not attribute. One is a book of drawings and etching stock-takes, and one is a cash book indexed by dealer. Beside them, the Whitney's Sanborn Hopper Archive holds some ninety notebooks of Josephine Hopper's, four of which have been digitised: a diary of the marriage as it bore on her own painting, two working files from the 1947 fight against New York University's attempt to evict the tenants of 3 Washington Square North, and a two-ring memorandum book of lists.

The ledgers have been used before. Gail Levin's catalogue raisonné was written from the record books and her biography from the diaries (Levin 1995a, 1995b); Deborah Lyons published the record books in 1997 (Lyons 1997); Madeleine Larson's typescript of the Provincetown diaries exists in five parts (Larson n.d.). What has not existed is a transcription of the digitised sheets that can be placed beside the photograph of each sheet and checked against it, with an apparatus that states what was read, what was doubted, what could not be read and whose hand wrote it. This article reports an experiment in producing such a transcription with a large language model as the first transcriber, under constraints designed to make its output checkable rather than trustworthy.

The objective is methodological. The Hopper corpus was chosen because it is the right size for a test, because the museum serves its photographs openly at a resolution at which Josephine Hopper's figures are legible, and because a ledger is a document in which errors are measurable: a price either adds up or it does not. The article therefore presents the corpus (Section 3), the transcription method and its rules (Section 4), the TEI encoding and the customisation that validates it (Section 5), the interface and the derived data that make the reading reproducible (Section 6), a discussion of what the method permits and what it does not (Section 7), and the same method run against three sibling corpora — two of mathematics, one a filmed lecture — which is where the editorial condition either generalises or fails (Section 8).

\section{Related work}

Four bodies of work bear on this experiment: account records in TEI, editions of working notebooks, the transcription of handwriting by large language models, and the evaluation of such models as producers of TEI. A fifth, the attribution of hands, is taken up in Section~7.

The TEI encoding of account books has a small but explicit literature. Tomasek and Bauman (2013), writing in this journal, proposed a stand-off model of financial records, the \q{transactionography}, in which transfers between parties are represented as data linked by pointers to the transcribed document rather than embedded in its markup, so that a transaction can be encoded once even where the document records it in several places. That distinction between the document and the transaction is the one the present edition adopts, with a different division of labour: the transcription encodes the document and nothing but the document, and every transaction is recovered from it by a script whose rules are published and whose output is regenerated on each build (Section 6). The present corpus is also unlike a double-entry book in that its writer kept no consistent form; the reader that recovers transactions from Book IV has to change its rule four times across the volume's fifty-four years, and Section 6.2 describes why.

Working notebooks have a literature of their own, and it is the nearest to this
corpus. Scholger (2020), in this journal, encodes the thirty-five notebooks of
the conceptual artist Hartmut Skerbisch --- some 2,100 inscribed pages, about
450 of them carrying drawings --- and argues that a plain \texttt{<graphic>}
loses what the drawing states: her model separates the graphical component, the
textual functions attached to it, and an interpretative layer addressed through
\texttt{@ana}, so that the stages by which a sketch became a work can be
followed across the corpus. The present edition does markedly less with its
drawings, and the difference is one of ambition rather than of means: Edward
Hopper's 205 record sketches are memoranda of finished pictures and not stages
of them, so each is marked as a figure, attributed, and left undescribed, on the
ground that a description of a drawing one pane away competes with looking at
it. Scholger's model is what this edition would need were the sketches read as
drawings.

The transcription of handwriting by large language models has moved quickly. Humphries and colleagues (2025) report that, on a corpus of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century English manuscripts, general-purpose language models achieved character error rates of about six to seven per cent, an improvement on specialised handwritten text recognition software; Crosilla, Klic and Colavizza (2025) benchmark several models on the same task and discuss the conditions under which the models' linguistic priors help and hinder. The present experiment differs in its object and its measure: not running prose but ruled tables of figures, names and abbreviations, where a language model's prior is a liability, since a plausible name is not a correct one and a rounded figure is a false transaction; and not an error rate against a ground truth, which does not exist for these sheets, but the reconciliation of the transcribed figures with the sums the writer pencilled at the foot of the leaves.

The evaluation of language models as producers of TEI is more recent. Strutz (2026) proposes a multi-dimensional framework for assessing model-generated TEI, from syntactic validity to semantic accuracy, on historical correspondence. The present edition does not ask the model to produce TEI at all: it writes a restricted language of sixteen macros from which the TEI is derived mechanically, so that the encoding's validity is a property of the converter and a difference between the two files is a bug rather than a judgement. Section 5 sets out the reasoning and Section 7 its cost.

First, three numbering systems overlap on every sheet and only one of them is an address. The Hoppers wrote leaf numbers on the paper; the museum's digitisation counts photographs, covers and versos included, so that it agrees with the leaf numbers nowhere; and the museum's ResourceSpace holds a resource reference that is in no order but that names exactly one image. \q{Leaf 58} names six different things across the six volumes and three photographs within Book I alone. A citation from the edition therefore prints both the leaf, which is what a reader of the photograph can see, and the reference, which is the only thing the museum can be asked about.

Second, the volumes were written by two people and annotated by more. The inside front cover of Book I states the division: \q{Recorded by Jo N Hopper (Mrs. Edward Hopper) at time each work is finished, or before it leaves studio. Drawings in the 3 books done by Edward Hopper.} The size in the title line of each work is the one figure Edward Hopper wrote; everything else in the work books is hers. Book IV is in a hand the edition cannot place. A transcription that flattened the hands would destroy the archive's chief documentary value, and the attribution of hands, which most transcription apparatus treats as optional, is accordingly the one element this method treats as mandatory.

Third, the colour of ink carries information. In Book IV, from leaf 7 onward, charges are written in black, receipts in red, and the sums the writer made herself in pencil; on the etching leaves of Book I, the writer states on the index leaf that ink marks the date a cheque was received and pencil the date a picture was sold. A transcription in one typeface loses this unless it records the colour, and a language model reading a photograph does not reliably distinguish it (Section 7).

\subsection{The four notebooks}

The four notebooks digitised by the Whitney are listed in Table~\ref{tab:notebooks}. They are not of one kind. \emph{Garrulities + Grouch} is a diary of twenty-two entries, eighteen of them dated, between 1927 and 1958, with the dates in the margin and the entries not in chronological order. \emph{Re: 3 Wash Sq} is a working file of drafts and a chronicle, in which the drafts were written from the front of the pad and the chronicle from the back, so that the two meet in the middle and the chronicle runs backwards through the photographs. \emph{Battle of Wash Sq.} is a memorandum book of eight sheets. The black two-ring notebook is a book of lists, whose dated content runs from 1937 to 1964 and whose subject changes every page or two. Their encoding required one element the ledgers do not: a dated entry, with the writer's date and the transcriber's assigned date kept apart.

\subsection{Provenance and access}

The three work books and Book V were the gift of Lloyd Goodrich, who organised the Whitney's Hopper retrospectives of 1950 and 1964; Book IV and the dealers' book were purchases. The notebooks came with the Sanborn Hopper Archive in 2017. Twenty-two further diaries of 1933 to 1956 are at the Provincetown Art Association and Museum, which publishes Madeleine Larson's typescript of them; those are not transcribed here and are used only through the typescript, cited by part and page.

The edition stores no photograph. The Whitney's download endpoint answers cross-origin requests and honours byte ranges; the interface points an image element at the museum's file, fetched as it is looked at, and the repository refuses image files. The inventory of sheets was harvested by a person in a browser, because the museum's listing pages sit behind a bot-check that the edition does not circumvent, and the harvest is committed as text so that it can be reviewed in a diff.

\subsection{The schema, and its values here}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} gives the whole arrangement at a glance, and it is
stated generally because it was not designed for this corpus: the same five
stations and the same loop run the three sibling editions of Section~8, and
what distinguishes the four is only the values the stations take. A
\emph{witness} is what the holding institution serves, addressed by the
holder's own reference, and it is never committed. A \emph{reading} is the one
file that is written --- by the model first, by a person thereafter --- in a
language whose every construct is declared, in passes whose header records
the model and the date. The \emph{derivations} are everything else the
edition publishes, computed from the reading by scripts and regenerated on
every build. The \emph{verifications} are whatever a machine can check of
them: a schema, an arithmetic, the agreement of two readers. The
\emph{address} is the reading placed beside its witness, each sentence cited
by the holder's reference and the edition's dated pass. The loop is the
person, who compares the reading with the witness, corrects the reading, and
declares that the comparison was made.

The four invariants the caption states are each enforced by a script or a
rule in every edition, and they leave a corpus few things to set: what the
witness is and how it is addressed; whether the model is to read it, to read
and restate it, or to index a reading already made; the language and the
size of a pass; which derivations are worth publishing; which verifications
the material admits; and what the person's check consists of.
Table~\ref{tab:params} gives the four editions' values side by side.

Here the witness is the Whitney's photograph of a sheet, addressed by its
ResourceSpace reference and fetched by the browser as it is looked at; the
model reads it, twelve sheets to a pass, into a LaTeX of sixteen macros; the
derivations are the TEI, the typeset and rendered readings, and four censuses;
the verifications are the customised schema, the Lean certificates and the
regeneration on every push; and the address is the site, which cites a sheet,
a leaf and a pass separately. The person's file is empty. The rest of this
section takes the stations in turn.

\subsection{The unit of work and the model}

A batch is twelve sheets, read in one fresh model context, and written as a single LaTeX file whose header records the model, the date, and what the batch found difficult. The skill that performs the reading accepts three models, Claude Fable 5.1, Claude Fable 5 and Claude Opus 5, and refuses any other, on the ground that a pass made on a model nobody chose produces a file whose provenance is an accident. Every file records the model that read its sheets, so that provenance is a fact about the file and not about the session. Between 5 and 13 September 2026, Opus 5 read most of the ledgers and the first sittings of the notebooks; Fable 5.1 read the last sittings of the black notebook and the Battle of Wash Sq. Where the rule of one context per batch was not kept, the header says so: batches 3 and 4 of Book I shared a context.

\subsection{The rules}

The governing rule is stated in the edition's method and is specific to ledgers: a guessed figure is a sale that did not happen. An invented word in a letter is a bad reading; an invented price in a ledger is a transaction at a price nobody paid, to a buyer who never bought, and it will be cited, because a table looks like data in a way that prose does not. From that rule follow the others.

Nothing illegible is guessed. A passage that cannot be read is marked illegible, and the mark is never replaced by a plausible reading. A reading offered with doubt is marked doubtful. Nothing is expanded or normalised: \q{Inv.} stays \q{Inv.}, \q{30 - 1/3} stays as written, and a name spelt four ways in the archive stays spelt four ways, because a corrected name is a claim about who bought a picture that the leaf did not make. A key to the abbreviations and notations is published separately, and each of its entries must match a transcribed leaf or the build fails. What the writer struck out is kept and marked struck, since a crossing-out is often the more informative half of an entry. Marginal additions are transcribed as marginal, with their position. The transcriber's own observations are confined to a note element that is typographically and structurally distinct from the writer's text, so that a count over the corpus can exclude it; the materials census, for example, reads only the writer's words, because the transcriber's notes discuss materials constantly and a count that read them would report a rare colourman as common because somebody remarked on its rarity.

Hands are attributed to Josephine Hopper, to Edward Hopper, to a later writer, or to nobody. \emph{Unidentified} is a permitted answer and is, the method notes, the right one far more often than confidence suggests. The drawn lettering of the covers is unattributed; the whole of Book IV is unattributed; a pencil annotation on Book II leaf 46 in a heavier hand is attributed to nobody. The edition's own attributions rest on a document and not on letterforms (Section 7.2); where a model attributes a passage on the letterforms alone, its note says so.

There is one edition and no second register. The Grothendieck edition this method derives from (Section 8) carries a modernised text beside the diplomatic one; a ledger needs none, since its English is plain and its columns are already a table, and a second text would be a second artefact to keep in step.

\subsection{What the model wrote}

The model writes a restricted LaTeX defined in a preamble of sixteen macros (Table~\ref{tab:macros}). The restriction is deliberate. A converter that accepted arbitrary LaTeX would silently mangle what it did not understand, and a silently mangled ledger is a table of numbers that look right; the renderer accepts only the subset, with ordinary line breaks and spacing, and fails loudly outside it, naming the file, the line and the construct. It also refuses a resource reference that is not in the harvested inventory and a file that omits the rights notice.

The choice of LaTeX rather than XML as the model's output language was practical and is discussed in Section 7. Its consequence for encoding is that the model never writes an element name, an attribute or a namespace, and cannot therefore produce ill-formed or invalid markup; what it can do is misapply a macro, and the converter's checks and the schema's closed value lists are designed to catch the misapplications that have a structural signature.

\section{The TEI encoding}

\subsection{Why the TEI is derived and not written}

The TEI files are produced by a script from the transcriptions alone. The script re-reads no sheet, and the method requires that it must not: each macro maps onto a fixed TEI construct, the conversion is mechanical, and a difference between a transcription and its TEI is by construction a defect in the converter and not an editorial decision. This is the reverse of the usual division, in which the TEI is the source of record and a display is derived from it, and it was chosen for three reasons.

The first is the model. Asked to write TEI it does so fluently, and its fluency is the problem: well-formed markup, plausible structure, invented attribute values, and no structural signature to the failure. A sixteen-macro language has a small surface, each macro answers one editorial question, and a macro used wrongly is a visibly wrong answer rather than a subtly wrong element.

That decision has a precedent worth naming, because it makes the fourteen
macros an editorial move rather than an idiosyncrasy. Cummings and Willcox
(2013), digitising the Stationers' Register of 1557 to 1708 with a keying
company that charged by the kilobyte, defined \texttt{tei\_corset}: a
deliberately reduced customisation of thirty-four elements with abbreviated
names and constrained attribute values, not itself TEI-conformant, which XSLT
converted up into conformant TEI while promoting presentational features into
semantic markup. The shape is the same as this edition's and the motive is not: they shrank
the markup to lower a bill, these macros to bound what a model may write; the
consequence is shared, that an intermediate language must be converted by
something testable, and the test is the schema.

The second is the checker. The transcription is read by people beside the photograph, in a rendering produced from the same file, and a LaTeX source with sixteen macros is also readable in itself, as a diplomatic transcription in a printed edition is, and can be corrected with a text editor.

The third is validation. Because the TEI is derived, it can be validated on every build against a customisation that declares exactly the elements the converter emits, and a validation failure identifies a converter bug or a macro misuse without any ambiguity about whose judgement is at fault.

\subsection{The customisation}

The customisation is an ODD, \texttt{hopper.odd}, in the form the Guidelines define for one (TEI Consortium 2026), served at the URL every exported file names, from which both the schema and its documentation derive. It declares fifty-eight elements, and the fifty-eight are not a sample of TEI's six hundred but the exact reach of the conversion: every element exists because a macro maps onto it. The modules and elements included are shown in Table~\ref{tab:odd}. Seven attribute value lists are declared closed, so that a \texttt{@type} on a division, a figure, a head, a note or a term, and a \texttt{@rend} on a highlighted cell or a ruled row, is drawn from a list and not invented per file, and the validator refuses any other value. The value lists are: division types (\texttt{leaf}, \texttt{part}, \texttt{work}, \texttt{entry}); figure types (\texttt{record-sketch}, \texttt{clipping}); head types (\texttt{work}); note types (\texttt{editorial}); the keyword facets (\texttt{medium}, \texttt{place}, \texttt{work}, \texttt{person}, \texttt{dealer}, \texttt{collection}, \texttt{society}, \texttt{publication}, \texttt{prize}, \texttt{client}, \texttt{subject}, \texttt{feature}); renditions on a cell (\texttt{italic}, \texttt{bold}, \texttt{mono}, \texttt{ink-red}, \texttt{ink-pencil}, \texttt{ink-blue}); and the one rendition a row may carry (\texttt{rule-above}).

\subsection{Tables, ink, and the cash book}

The ruled tables are encoded as tables, one row per ruled line and one cell per column, with an empty label row where the leaf heads no column. The colour of ink, where the pass that reads it has run, is encoded as a rendition on the highlighted cell; a rule the writer drew across the money column above a row is a rendition on the row. \subsection{Validation}

The converter's output is validated in continuous integration against the schema derived from the ODD, and the workflow fails on any invalid file. The validator exists because of a defect it found: for as long as the export was checked only for well-formedness, every file in the corpus was well-formed XML and invalid TEI, since the converter had placed a head at each leaf inside a single division per batch, and TEI places a head only at the start of the division it heads. Nothing had said so because nothing had asked. The lesson is general and is recorded in the validator's own header: a deposit of unvalidated TEI is a deposit of XML.

\section{Interface and reproducibility}

\subsection{The reading beside the photograph}

The interface at \url{https://hopper.commutator.io} was built for this project. It renders the transcription in a left pane and the Whitney's photograph of the sheet in a right pane, and scrolling the transcription past a page-break element turns the photograph to that sheet, as facsimile viewers generally do; what is particular to it is that the photograph is the museum's own file, fetched as it is looked at and never stored, and the citation described below. Every doubtful figure can be checked against the hand it was read from without a second window. A Cite control on each sheet builds three citations from what the page already knows, a sheet, a leaf and a batch, each naming the museum's object clause and the edition's reading clause separately, and offers only those that exist: a sheet the Hoppers did not number gets no leaf citation, because inventing a number for the sake of a tidy citation would invent a fact about the object. The citation names the model and the date of the reading, because a citation of this edition cites a reading and not a fact, and a reading that could not be dated could not be superseded.

The reading views for the notebooks work the same way and are addressed by the museum's reference rather than by a page number, since an opening photographed whole carries two page numbers and a cover none.

The interface was built before the transcription was finished, and the order
matters, because a reading that can be seen beside its witness is a reading
that can be debugged. A misread cell in a file of ruled rows is invisible: it
is a well-formed number in a well-formed column. It becomes visible in two
places the site provides, the pane where it stands beside the photograph, and
the drawings the derivations make of it, where a wrong figure is an outlier
and a wrong year a hollow. Most of the corrections the repository records
were found in the second place and settled in the first. The rule that a red
figure in Book~IV is money received, applied to the volume's opening leaves,
turned eleven prices into receipts; the rise showed in the accounts before it
showed in any file, and reading leaf 7 beside its photograph showed the
convention beginning on that leaf's last line. Three ruled rows under one
title on Book~I leaf 80 read as three sales until the leaf, in the pane,
showed a sum written above them and the word \q{payment} in each; the
accounts now book one sale of 2500 paid in three parts. The absence of 1921
from the cash book was a hollow in the calendar before it was a year line
misread on leaf 53. None of this needs an elaborate interface, only the reading and the witness
on one screen and the derived figures on the next, from the first batch
onward; an edition that builds its reading view last finds its errors last.

\subsection{Derived data, and the rule that a build must fail}

Everything the interface shows beyond the transcription is derived from the transcription by a script whose header states what it counts and what it therefore is not, and the continuous integration workflow regenerates each derived file and fails if the committed copy differs. The manifest of what is transcribed is observed from the files, never declared, so that a progress figure cannot claim work that was not done; the only declared states, \emph{checked} and \emph{skipped}, are held in a separate file that is empty. The citation metadata is generated from a census of the files, on the ground that a hand-written sheet count is wrong from the next batch onward. The glossary of abbreviations and notations is hand-declared, but each entry carries a pattern that must match a transcribed leaf, and the search runs over the writer's words and never over a transcriber's note, so that an entry cannot cite as evidence the sentence written because of it.

The accounts reader recovers transactions from the ruled rows. It counts a row only where the leaf puts a price in one cell and a date in another, because the column a date stands in is what says whether it dates the sale or the cheque; on the etching leaves of Book I the first column dates the exhibition, so those rows are set aside rather than dated wrongly. Where a row states a price, a rate and a receipt, the receipt is checked against the price less the commission; of ninety-five such rows, ninety agree to the cent, and the five that do not are reported and not corrected. For Book IV the reader has a second set of rules, set out period by period in a method note, because the writer's form changes four times across the volume: items are the charge; a bill counts only where nothing was itemised since the last settlement; a bare figure is a subtotal where the arithmetic of the lines above it says so, since the leaf rules a line above a subtotal and the transcription does not record the rule; a \q{less} line is a deduction whichever thing it names; a title charged more than once with a payment on account is one picture at the largest figure. The check on the reader is the book's own pencil totals, and the reader's receipts reconcile to them to the cent wherever the leaf's arithmetic closes.

Two of the reader's rules were found by bugs, and the method note records them: an unanchored match on the word \emph{bill} took a story title, \q{Wild Bill in Deadwood Gulch}, for an invoice and lost a drawing of 1915; a header rule read \q{May 25th Initial Letters} as a new client and counted a bill of 225 twice. Both were caught by comparing per-year totals with leaves read by hand. A faithful transcription can surface a reader bug without containing one.

\subsection{The rules, stated as proofs}

A reader whose rules are written in prose and applied by a script can drift from them unnoticed, and a total can reconcile by accident. The derived accounts are therefore also restated in the Lean 4 theorem prover, in a directory of the repository that depends on nothing but Lean, so that the continuous integration build fails if the script and its stated rules disagree. The formalisation was written by Claude Opus 5 under the author's direction, on 14 September 2026. Every figure in the ledgers is dollars and cents and every commission a third, a quarter, a tenth or fifteen per cent, so money is represented as integers in sixtieths of a cent, where all of them are exact. General lemmas are proved for every input by decision procedures for linear arithmetic; the data is checked by evaluation in the kernel (\texttt{decide +kernel}) and never by compiled code; and warnings are errors, so an unfinished proof fails the build.

Two certificates are proved. The first concerns the accounts as published: each activity's years are recomputed from its rows, and the identity that what accrued equals what was collected plus what is outstanding plus what was not recorded is derived from the rows rather than read off the totals; the reader's two-cent tolerance is proved to accept every figure written to the cent after a truncated third and to reject a slip of a third of a dollar; the ninety-five checkable rows are proved to yield ninety agreements and exactly the five disagreements the reader reports; and each hand-declared pencil total is proved equal to the reader's receipts for that year plus the declared omissions. The second concerns the Book IV reader. Its four passes are stated as Lean functions, and the properties the method note asserts in prose are proved of them for every input: a bill is charged only where nothing was itemised since the last settlement, so no commission is counted twice; a \q{less} line is never a charge; a bare figure is a subtotal exactly when the answering line or the arithmetic says so; a picture paid in instalments is charged once; the year does not carry across a gap between batches. The kernel then evaluates the functions over all 3,281 transcribed rows of the volume and proves that each row is decided exactly once and that the Lean reader and the JavaScript reader agree on every row's kind, year and disposition: the JavaScript exports the lexical features it recognised and the decisions it took, Lean recomputes the decisions from the features, and altering a single disposition in the export fails the build.

The exercise refined one rule, since the proof required the exception that a \q{less} line which is also a ditto immediately under a receipt reads as that receipt, and it turned a known gap into a theorem: the absence of 1921 from the cash book follows from a year line on leaf 53 that reads 1920 over entries of 1921, and the corresponding theorem is written to fail once that line is corrected at source. The limits are those of the method as a whole. The certificates say nothing about a reading, and a figure misread consistently in the rows and in the sums would pass; the regular expressions that recognise a row's words are inputs, not proved; and what is proved is that the script agrees with its stated rules, not that the rules are the right interpretation of the writer's practice. The certificates are checked on every push by the workflow that builds the site, and an equivalent GitLab CI configuration is provided.

A more systematic comparison exists for one volume and shows the limit of the reading. The consequence is bounded and can be stated: a missing line changes a sum, so the cash-book figures are floors in a second sense, and no such pass has run on the other volumes or the notebooks, about whose omissions nothing is therefore known.

\subsection{Limits of the model as a transcriber and as an encoder}

The experiment identified five limits, none of them a matter of error rate.

The model does not see colour reliably. Book IV's meaning is in its ink, and fourteen batches were transcribed without recording it; the colour was recovered afterwards by an image-processing script that classifies each money cell and writes the result into the transcription, on the ground that colour is the one thing on the leaf a machine can see better than the hand it is written in. That recovery is partial and its extent should be stated: on 20 September 2026 thirty-two of Book IV's one hundred and sixty-one sheets carry an ink mark, the pass having been run where a leaf's rows and the written lines of its photograph align exactly, and stopped where they do not. The stop is mechanical rather than palaeographic, so the remaining leaves await the human reading rather than a better classifier; where the convention itself begins, and how that was found, is told in Section 6.1. A method that depended on the language model for the ink would have produced a cash book in which a charge and a receipt cannot be told apart.

The model carries context forward as a rule, and rules fail at boundaries. The year of a Book IV entry is stated once, on a line of its own, and carried down the leaf; the year line of leaf 53 (Section 6.1) is the case, where the transcription keeps the line as written and the reader carries the wrong year. That is the intended behaviour of both, and it is wrong, and it is listed for correction at source rather than repaired in the derived data.

The model reads names as language. The archive spells Crowninshield three ways — Crowninshield, Crownenshield, Crowneshield — and Saltillo two, and the model, left to itself, would settle them. The rule that nothing is normalised holds it to the leaf; the discipline is the rule's and not the model's.

The model is fluent in TEI, and that fluency is the hazard Section 5.1 describes. The decision not to let the model write TEI at all, and to derive the TEI mechanically from a sixteen-macro language it can hardly misuse, is the central encoding decision of the edition. Its cost is that the encoding is only as expressive as the macro set, and the macro set was designed for what these ten books contain; the transactionography of Tomasek and Bauman (2013), for instance, has no place in the transcription and lives entirely in the reader.

The model is confident about hands. The attribution of a hand on the letterforms is the one judgement in the apparatus that no arithmetic can check, and the model attributes readily. That difficulty is not the model's alone: Stokes (2015) argues that computational measures of similarity cannot on their own establish that two specimens were written by one person, since there is rarely any independent ground truth to verify the claim against, and that such output should direct an expert's attention rather than stand as a verdict. The rule that \emph{unidentified} is a permitted answer, and the decision to attribute the whole of Book IV --- a hand that predates the marriage by eleven years --- to nobody, are the method's response. Where the edition does attribute, it does so on a document and not on the letterforms: the 205 record sketches of Books I, II and III carry \texttt{@hand} because the inside front cover of Book I declares, in Josephine Hopper's writing, that the drawings in the three books are Edward Hopper's, and the twenty-four sketches of Book V carry none, because that sentence does not name Book V. A human palaeographer with the physical volumes would do better than either, and the edition says so on the leaf.

\subsection{Human validation}

The edition distinguishes three states of a batch, and only one of them is observed from the files. \emph{Drafted} is what a file's existence proves. \emph{Skipped}, for a batch judged to hold nothing to transcribe, and \emph{checked}, for a batch that a person has compared with the photographs sheet by sheet, are declared in a status file, and that file declares nothing for any batch. The reason is stated in its comment: a hand-kept status table drifts optimistic and nobody notices, so the two states no file can prove are the only two a person may write, and the site shows a transcribed batch as drafted because that is observed rather than declared.

What a human check consists of has been defined as an open question of the edition rather than assumed. The protocol under discussion is the one this article's comparison table performs in miniature: a person opens the reading beside the photograph, reads each transcribed line against the sheet, corrects the transcription at source with a note that names who checked it, and only then may the batch be declared checked; the citation of every sheet in that batch then changes to say that a person has gone through it, and only a person may declare that. Until then, every citation of the edition names a machine pass and a date, and the address of the sheet does not move when the reading does.

\subsection{What the method permits, and what it does not}

The method permits a reading of a seven-hundred-sheet archive to be produced in days, cited by leaf and reference, exported as valid TEI, and exposed in full to correction beside its source; its derived data to be regenerated under published rules and checked against the writer's own sums; those rules to be proved of the script that applies them; and the whole to be held in one repository whose every change is a dated commit, so that a citation names a version.

It does not produce a checked transcription, and does not claim to. It does not see ink, does not attribute hands with authority, does not encode transactions, and cannot produce a text more expressive than its macro language. And it does not remove the need for the person it was designed around: the reader beside the photograph, whose correction is the only event that changes a batch's state.

What the three siblings taught the method is specific in each case. The
Grothendieck fonds carries a second register the ledgers refuse: its
transcriptions come in pairs, a diplomatic file and a modernised one (124 and 53
files), because mathematical notation of 1960 is not read today as it was
written, whereas \q{30 - 1/3} means in 2026 what it meant in 1927 — the decision
against a second edition here (Section 4.3) is a decision this project had to
take twice, in opposite directions. The Gallica corpus made the address itself
the unit: a volume enters only where the library serves the whole of it, and a
manuscript that Gallica merely references on a partner's site is in no table and
no thumbnail, which is the same rule this edition states about documents with no
public digitisation. And the morphology course made the witness something other
than an image.

That last case is the one that generalises the claim. Jean-François Debord's course is
seventy-seven hours of speech; the first transcription is not the model's but
the platform's automatic captions, and the model's work is an index — 95 terms,
73 references and 139 digressions, each carrying the second at which the thing
is said. The repository publishes no caption and no video: only video
identifiers, term identifiers and offsets in seconds, the captions themselves
staying out of version control, as the photographs stay out of this one. The
editorial condition is unchanged and its instrument moves: a reader does not
compare the reading with a photograph but with the recording, at the timestamp
given, by listening. What every corpus here has in common is not a document
type; it is that no sentence is asserted which a reader cannot address.

The platform corpus also shows where the condition begins to fail, and it is
worth stating before such corpora multiply. A sheet at the Whitney will answer
to the same reference in twenty years; a video can be withdrawn, re-edited, or
have its captions regenerated by a newer recogniser that yields different words
at different seconds. The address survives and what it serves does not, and
nothing in the citation announces the change. Three things follow, none of them
solved here, and the conclusion returns to them: such a citation must carry the
date it was consulted and a fingerprint of what was consulted — a hash of the
caption file, a duration, a count of segments — to prove that the witness moved
without republishing it; a verification of this kind is revocable, and an
interface that keeps a dead timestamp on screen asserts what it can no longer
support; and a platform forbids the mirror while promising no permanence. An
edition may not copy what nobody undertakes to keep.

\section{Conclusion}

This article has described an edition in which a language model is the first transcriber and the editorial apparatus is designed to make its reading checkable rather than trustworthy. The method rests on four decisions: a restricted transcription language whose macros answer one editorial question each; a mechanical derivation of TEI from that language, validated on every build against a customisation that declares exactly what the converter emits; a derivation of all counts and figures from the transcription under published rules, checked against the writer's own sums and, for the accounts, certified in Lean against the rules as stated; and an interface that places the reading beside the photograph and cites every sheet by the museum's own reference and the edition's own dated pass.

What the experiment opens is a division of labour. The model reads; the scripts derive; the schema validates; and the person checks, corrects at source, and declares. The first three have been done for this corpus; the fourth has not, and the edition's status file, which declares nothing, measures where the method stops. The corpus is deposited under a persistent identifier. The human check that turns a drafted batch into a checked one is to be settled with the scholars who will do the reading, is tracked as an issue of the repository, and is the one on which every claim in this article depends.

What it also opens is the range of what can now be read. The four corpora of
Section~8 are all archives in the ordinary sense --- ledgers, manuscripts,
working papers, a filmed course --- and between them they already cross the
line that used to bound this kind of work: three are written and one is spoken,
three are held by institutions that publish facsimiles and one is held by a
platform that publishes none. Behind that line lies the larger part of what the
twentieth and twenty-first centuries have left: recorded lectures and
interviews, radio and television archives, the video collections of national
libraries and government agencies, and the vast material of social platforms
--- posts, threads, comments, the captions that accompany millions of hours of
speech. Most of it has never been read in the sense this article means, because
reading it by hand was never conceivable.

The method described here does not become easier on that material; it becomes
more necessary, and its conditions become harder to meet, in the three ways
Section~8 sets out: the address may outlive what it serves, the rights forbid
the mirror while promising no permanence, and the first transcription is often
the platform's rather than the editor's, which turns the model from the first
reader into the second and makes the provenance statement more important rather
than less.

None of that changes the condition this article has argued for. An edition is
worth what its reader can check, whether the checking is done by looking at a
photograph, by adding up a column, or by listening at a timestamp. What changes
is how much there is to read, and how badly it needs somebody to say, of each
piece of it, who read it and on what day.
