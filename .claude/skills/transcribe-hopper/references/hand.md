# The two hands

What passes over these sheets have learned, so that the next pass does not
derive it again at the cost of the attention it was meant to save.

**This narrows a field of candidates and settles nothing.** Where the sheet
will not support what is written here, the sheet wins and `\ill{}` stands.

Written from Book I, batch 1 (sheets 1–12), 5 September 2026, Opus 5. Add to it
after every batch; say which batch taught you what.

---

## Josephine Nivison Hopper

She wrote almost every word in the six volumes, over fifty-four years, so
"her hand" is really four or five hands and the differences are datable.

**The running hand of the 1920s entries** is small, upright, quick, with the
letters barely joined. It is legible at the Whitney's `pre` size (1292 × 2000)
without magnification for prose, and **not** for figures: tile before reading a
column.

**Letters that mislead:**

- **Her terminal `s` is a small open loop** that reads as `o` at the end of a
  word. « Etchings » repeatedly looks like « Etchingo ». Read the word, not the
  letter.
- **`r` and `n` are near-identical** in the running hand. « Sterner » and
  « Sterney » are not distinguishable letter by letter; it is Sterner because
  the name recurs a dozen times.
- **`th` in a date ordinal is a single stroke with a loop** — « 28th » looks
  like « 28h ». She drops the ordinal entirely as often as she writes it.
- **Her `2` and her `7`** are distinguished only by the length of the upper
  stroke, and in the price columns they are the two figures that matter most.
  This is the single commonest reason to reach for `\uncertain{}`.

**Abbreviations she uses without expanding, and which are not errors:**

| As written | Means |
|---|---|
| `Inv.` | invited (to an exhibition, as against submitted to a jury) |
| `A` / `R` | accepted / refused, in the column beside the date |
| `Ex.` | exhibition |
| `Bklyn.` | Brooklyn |
| `N. A. D.` | National Academy of Design |
| `Com.` / `com` | commission |
| `Reprod.` | reproduced |
| `Rec'd` | received |
| `Gal.` | gallery |
| `Mus.` | museum |
| `E. H.` | Edward Hopper |
| `J. H.` | herself |

**Names that recur, and their spellings on the leaf.** She is inconsistent, and
the inconsistency is transcribed:

- **Keppel** — Frederick Keppel & Co., the print dealers. Spelt `Kepple` at
  least once (Book I leaf 3, 4 November 1940). Leave it.
- **Rehn** — Frank K. M. Rehn, his dealer from 1924. Also `Rehn Gal.`,
  `Rehn Gallery`.
- **Weyhe** — E. Weyhe, the bookshop and print gallery.
- **Kennedy** — Kennedy & Co.
- **Downtown Gal.** — Edith Halpert's Downtown Gallery; she also writes
  `Halpert`.
- **Vicery Atkins & Torey** — for Vickery, Atkins & Torrey, San Francisco.
  Misspelled on the leaf. Leave it.
- **Zigrosser** — Carl Zigrosser, Philadelphia Museum.
- **Mrs. Albert Sterner**, **Mrs. Mario Sterner** — both forms appear.

**Her numbers.** Prices are dollars with the sign written above and left of the
figure, often detached from it, and easy to attach to the wrong column.
Commissions are `30 - 1/3` (price 30, one third to the dealer) or `25 - 1/10`.
The vulgar fractions are raised-over-lowered and are transcribed as `2/3`.
Receipts in the rightmost columns are decimal — `16.66`, `12.60`, `18.70`.

**Her red pencil** rings a figure where one line records two prints rather than
one, and underlines a total. It is hers, it is not a correction, and it does
not survive into the transcription except as a `\note{}` where it changes what
a row means.

## Edward Hopper

He wrote very little, and what he wrote is easy: **drawn lettering**, upright,
spaced, closer to a title block than to handwriting. On the etchings leaves it
is one line — the title and the plate size, `Evening Wind   7 x 8 3/8"`.

**The trap is attributing the covers to him.** The front cover of Book I and
its title page carry « Book I. » and « Edward Hopper / His Work » in drawn
lettering that resembles his and is not obviously his. Batch 1 records both as
`\hand{unidentified}` with a note, and that is the standing recommendation
until somebody has compared them against a signed drawing.

His ink record sketches are not writing and are not transcribed. `\sketch{}`
records that one stands there, and carries only what is *written* on it.

## Later hands

At least one, after 1967, in ballpoint. Book I leaf 1 carries « Collection of
Philadelphia Mus. … 1962 » and « inside front cover - Feb. 14, 1961 » in a
different ink and a rounder hand than the entries around them; the 1961 note
is in her hand, late, and the 1962 one may not be. Where a line cannot be
placed, `\hand{unidentified}`.

## Marks that are not letters

- **A pencil tick** to the left of a title in the leaf-1 plate list. Its sense
  is not established. Do not transcribe it as a letter; note it once.
- **Small numerals** to the left of the same titles — 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 — not in
  the order of the list. Sense not established.
- **`X`** beside « American Landscape ». Not established.
- **A fine vertical line bracketing three consecutive rows** — Book I leaf 5,
  the entries of February 1957. It does not delete the words; it appears to
  group them as one transaction. Transcribe the rows and do not interpret the
  bracket.
- **Ditto marks** — `"` repeated across a row, sometimes four or five deep.
  Transcribe them as she writes them, `" \quad " \quad "`. Expanding them would
  assert that the row below repeats the row above in every column, which is
  what the marks mean on some leaves and not on others.

## What the clippings do

Halftone reproductions are pasted or hinged over the **top third** of several
etchings leaves, and they cover text. On Book I leaf 2 the clipping covers the
price and buyer columns for every row from January 1921 to December 1922; on
leaf 4 it covers the exhibitions column for two years and the whole of the
price columns above May 1923.

**A covered line is `\ill{}`, and the `\note{}` says it is under paper.** A
reader must be able to tell "nobody could read this" from "she left it blank",
because the second is information about the transaction and the first is
information about the photograph.

Some clippings are hinged rather than pasted, and the Whitney photographed the
same opening twice — once with the clipping down and once turned back. The two
states together establish which leaf it is attached to, and neither does alone.
That is worth a `\note{}` and is not worth more.
